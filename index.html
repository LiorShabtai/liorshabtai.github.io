Working Papers

The Unintended Consequences of Carbon Markets

PDF | SSRN

Abstract. This paper shows that carbon markets inadvertently weaken incentives for green innovation in hard-to-abate industries by allowing firms to rely on purchasing emission allowances rather than independently decarbonizing. A triple-difference analysis of the European Union Emissions Trading System (EU-ETS) finds that the rise in green patenting is concentrated among easy-to-abate firms, with no comparable increase among hard-to-abate firms. A directed technical change model of a carbon market with sectoral differences in abatement costs shows that carbon markets inefficiently direct most innovation efforts toward decarbonizing the easy-to-abate sector. Socially optimal sector-specific research subsidies redirect innovation to support a clean transition in the hard-to-abate sector and increase welfare by 3.8% in consumption-equivalent terms. These subsidies need to be nearly three times as large (in present value) as those provided to innovators in the easy-to-abate sector.

Work in Progress

The Decline of Productivity-Enhancing R&D

with Efraim Benmelech

Abstract. We show that the changing composition of corporate R&D can help explain slower productivity growth despite rising R&D spending. Classifying U.S. public firms’ R&D over 1963–2022 using input–output tables, we document a shift toward consumer-oriented innovation and away from process innovation after around 2000. Following four decades of relative stability, the consumer share doubled and the process share fell 40 percent. Industry-level analysis shows that only process R&D has a positive and significant relation with measured TFP growth. A counterfactual retaining the 2000 composition of R&D implies 37 percent higher average annual TFP growth over 2001–2021, without additional spending. Most of the difference reflects reallocation toward consumer-oriented industries.
